# Lesson 4: Unit economics

**Phase 1 · Week 1 · Day 4** · 25 minutes

## Why this matters

A ROAS of 2 can be a great result for one business and a disaster for another. The
difference is profit margin. Unit economics tells you how much you can afford to pay for a
customer. Without it, you can't say whether any campaign is working.

## Key ideas

### Revenue is not profit

When a customer pays $36 for a bag of coffee, the business doesn't keep $36. It pays for the
beans, roasting, packaging, shipping, and payment fees. Together these are the **cost of goods
sold (COGS)**.

```
Gross profit  = revenue − COGS
Gross margin  = gross profit ÷ revenue
```

If the bag costs $21.60 to make and ship, gross profit is $14.40 and gross margin is 40%.

### Break-even ROAS

You break even on ads when ad spend equals gross profit.

```
Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin
```

| Gross margin | Break-even ROAS |
|---|---|
| 20% | 5.0 |
| 40% | 2.5 |
| 60% | 1.67 |
| 80% | 1.25 |

Low-margin businesses need a very high ROAS. Software and services, with high margins, can
grow with a much lower one.

### Break-even CPA

```
Break-even CPA = AOV × gross margin
```

AOV is average order value. With an AOV of $36 and a 40% margin, you can pay up to $14.40 for
a sale before you lose money on it.

### Target CPA and target ROAS

Break-even means zero profit. Most businesses want some profit per order:

```
Target CPA  = break-even CPA − profit wanted per order
Target ROAS = AOV ÷ target CPA
```

If Pebble wants $4 profit per first order: target CPA = $14.40 − $4 = $10.40, and target ROAS
= $36 ÷ $10.40 = 3.46.

### Lifetime value changes everything

Many customers buy more than once. **Lifetime value (LTV)** is the gross profit one customer
brings over their whole relationship with you:

```
LTV = AOV × orders per year × years as a customer × gross margin
```

If a Pebble subscriber buys 6 bags a year and stays 1 year: LTV = $36 × 6 × 1 × 0.40 =
**$86.40**. Pebble could pay much more than $14.40 to win a subscriber and still make money
over time. That's why subscription businesses often lose money on the first order on purpose.

The risk: LTV is a forecast. If customers cancel sooner than expected, the maths breaks. Base
it on real repeat-purchase data when you have it.

### CAC and payback

- **CAC (customer acquisition cost):** All marketing spend ÷ new customers. Like CPA, but
  counts only new customers and often includes more costs.
- **LTV to CAC ratio:** A common rule of thumb says 3 or more is healthy.
- **Payback period:** How many months of gross profit it takes to earn back the CAC. Shorter
  is safer, because cash runs out before profit arrives.

### Lead generation economics

With leads, the sale happens later. Work backwards:

```
Value of one lead = gross profit per customer × close rate
```

Brightsmile Dental earns $600 gross profit from a typical new patient in their first year. One
in four leads becomes a patient. Each lead is worth $600 × 0.25 = **$150**. That's the
break-even cost per lead.

### Discounts change your break-even

A discount comes straight out of margin. Your costs stay the same while revenue drops. Always
recalculate break-even ROAS when you run a sale. Practice 4 shows how big the effect is.

## Common mistakes

- **Using revenue instead of profit:** "ROAS of 2 means we doubled our money" is false unless
  margins are 100%.
- **Forgetting shipping and fees in COGS:** They often turn a profitable campaign into a loss.
- **Using a guessed LTV to justify any CPA:** Use real repeat rates, and be conservative.
